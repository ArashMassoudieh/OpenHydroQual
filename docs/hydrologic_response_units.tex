\title{\textbf{Hydrologic Response Units in OpenHydroQual}\\[0.3em]
       \large The HRU and Urban HRU composite blocks}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\vspace{-2.5em}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A hydrologic response unit (HRU) in OpenHydroQual is a composite block that bundles the surface,
soil and groundwater stores of one piece of land and the connectors between them, so that a
sub-catchment is added to a model as a single object with a single set of properties. This document
describes the two HRUs: the \code{Hydrologic\_Response\_Unit} (``HRU''), a single infiltrating surface
over a three-layer soil column and a groundwater cell, and the \code{Urban\_HRU}, which divides the
land into pervious and impervious surfaces with their own routing reaches, places the groundwater cell
under the whole unit as an unconfined aquifer whose initial state is a water-table elevation,
recharges it through the unsaturated conductivity of the bottom soil layer, can draw
evapotranspiration from the deeper soil layers through a second source, takes soil parameters layer
by layer and carries three calibration multipliers. For each unit the document sets out the members and connectors, the equation of each
flux, how the plan area is divided, how the vertical geometry and the initial state follow from a
few properties, and how the impervious fraction should be estimated, and it closes with a worked
example and a property reference.
\end{abstract}

Use the \textbf{HRU} for land without significant impervious cover --- forest, fields, parks,
hillslopes --- and for cascades of units where the overland flow of one unit runs onto the next
(\cref{sec:hru}). Use the \textbf{Urban HRU} wherever part of the land is impervious and drains
directly to a stream or storm drain: it partitions the area, routes each surface separately, and its
subsurface (\cref{sec:urbanspec}) stays physically sensible when the water table lies well below the
soil column.

\section{Elements shared by both units}
\label{sec:shared}
Both units build their subsurface from the same members: three \code{Soil} layers and a
\code{Groundwater cell}, connected by infiltration, two percolation links and a recharge link. The
geometry and the soil-water relations below apply to both; the units differ in the plan areas they
give these members, the recharge link and the groundwater storage (\cref{sec:hru,sec:urbanspec}).

\subsection{Vertical geometry}
The user gives three levels; everything else is derived:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Member & Thickness & Bottom elevation \\
\midrule
\code{Catchment}, \code{Impervious\_Catchment}, reaches & --- & $z_s$ (\code{surface\_elevation}) \\
\code{Soil\_1} & $D/7$ & $z_s-D/7$ \\
\code{Soil\_2} & $2D/7$ & $z_s-3D/7$ \\
\code{Soil\_3} & $4D/7$ & $z_s-D$ \\
\code{Groundwater} & $b$ (\code{groundwater\_thickness}) & $z_s-D-b$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent $D$ is \code{depth\_to\_groundwater}. The $1\!:\!2\!:\!4$ split puts the thinnest layer at
the surface, where moisture gradients are steepest. The groundwater cell sits directly below the soil
column.

\subsection{Soil water}
Each soil layer stores water $V=\theta A_\ell d_\ell$ and has the van Genuchten--Mualem relations
\begin{equation}
  S_e=\frac{\theta-\theta_r}{\theta_s-\theta_r},\qquad
  \psi=-\frac1\alpha\big(S_e^{-1/m}-1\big)^{1/n},\qquad
  k_r=\Big[1-\big(1-S_e^{1/m}\big)^m\Big]^2,\qquad m=1-\frac1n,
\end{equation}
with $S_e$ bounded to $[0.001,0.999]$, the suction $|\psi|$ capped at $10^4$\,m, and the total head
$h=z_{\text{bottom}}+d_\ell+\psi$ (plus a small excess pressure above saturation, governed by the
specific storage). The layer's conductivity is $K_{sat}=$ \code{K\_sat\_original} $\times$
\code{K\_sat\_scale\_factor}.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,style=nextline]
\item[Infiltration] (\code{surfacewater\_to\_soil\_link}, surface $\to$ \code{Soil\_1}):
  $Q=\min(A_s,A_1)\,K_{sat,1}\,(h_s-h_1)/(d_1/2)\;\cdot\,d/(d+0.001)$, i.e.\ Darcy flow over half the
  top layer, switched off smoothly when no water is ponded.
\item[Percolation] (\code{soil\_to\_soil\_link}): $Q=\bar A\,K_{\text{eff}}\,(h_i-h_j)/\bar d$ with
  $K_{\text{eff}}=\min(K_{sat,i},K_{sat,j})\,k_r\big(\max(S_{e,i},S_{e,j})\big)$, mean area $\bar A$ and
  mean thickness $\bar d$. Flow can be upward (capillary rise).
\item[Evapotranspiration] is withdrawn from \code{Soil\_1} by the source connected to the
  \code{Evapotranspiration} property. With the \code{Evapotranspiration\_Time\_Series (Soil)} source
  the rate is $\text{corr\_fact}\times ET_0\times A_1\,\theta/\theta_s$. The Urban HRU can also withdraw
  from \code{Soil\_2} and \code{Soil\_3} through a second source (\cref{sec:et2}).
\end{description}

\section{HRU}
\label{sec:hru}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{hru_structure}
\caption{\code{Hydrologic\_Response\_Unit}: members, internal links and ports.}
\label{fig:hru}
\end{figure}

The HRU (\file{hydrologic\_response\_unit.json}, \cref{fig:hru}) has five members --- a
\code{Catchment} and the shared soil column and groundwater cell --- and four internal links. All of
them receive the whole plan area $A$. It has no internal reaches: the surface drains through its
\code{Catchment\_link} port directly to whatever block it is linked to, which makes cascades of units
possible (the overland flow of an upslope unit runs onto the surface of the next). Its ports:

Port (link type) & From member & Typical target & Icon \\

\code{groundwater\_link} & \code{Groundwater} & the groundwater cell of a neighbouring unit & \raisebox{-0.4\height}{\includegraphics[height=0.9cm]{Groundwater_link.png}} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Baseflow can leave the groundwater cell through either of two connectors (use one of them
for a given discharge pathway, not both in parallel):
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,style=nextline]
\item[\code{groundwater\_to\_stream}] Darcy flow driven by the head difference between the
  groundwater cell and the receiving segment, $Q=K\,A_b\,(h_g-h_c)/L$, with the connector's
  exchange area $A_b$, flow path $L$ and conductivity $K$. Discharge stops, and reverses, when the
  stream stage rises above the water table.
\item[\code{Linear\_baseflow}] A linear reservoir above an inactive storage (\file{groundwater.json}):
  \begin{equation}
    Q = k\,\big(\theta_g-\theta_{\min}\big)_+\,V_g^{\text{tot}},
  \end{equation}
  where $\theta_g$ is the moisture content and $V_g^{\text{tot}}$ the total volume of the groundwater
  cell, $k$ the \code{recession\_rate} (1/d) and $\theta_{\min}$ the \code{min\_moisture\_content}
  below which no baseflow leaves (an absolute moisture content, not larger than the porosity). With
  no recharge the active storage recedes exponentially with time constant $1/k$. The discharge does
  not depend on the stream stage or on \code{groundwater\_K}; $k$ is set, or calibrated, directly
  from observed recession rates. An example is in
  \exfile{Examples/Linear\_Baseflow/Linear\_Baseflow.ohq}.
\end{description}

\noindent Recharge uses the stock \code{soil2groundwater\_link} with the constant conductivity
\code{recharge\_K},
\begin{equation}
  Q_r=A\,K_{rech}\,\frac{h_3-h_g}{L},\qquad L=\tfrac12\,(d_3+b),
\end{equation}
and the groundwater member is the stock \code{Groundwater cell}, whose head is
$h_g=z_g+b+(\theta_g-\phi)/S_s$ with user-supplied porosity $\phi$ and specific storage $S_s$, and whose
initial state is a moisture content $\theta_{g,0}$. With the defaults ($S_s=0.01$\,m$^{-1}$) the cell
responds as a \emph{confined} aquifer: a change of 0.01 in moisture content moves the head by 1\,m;
$S_s=\phi/b$ gives unconfined behaviour, and $\theta_{g,0}=\phi+(h_0-z_g-b)\,S_s$ starts the water table
at $h_0$. A constant recharge conductivity is
adequate when the water table lies at or just below the soil column; when it lies far below, see
\cref{sec:recharge}. One set of soil parameters applies to all three layers. A two-unit hillslope
using the HRU is in \exfile{Examples/Hydrologic\_Response\_Unit/HRU\_hillslope.ohq}.

\section{Urban HRU}
\label{sec:urbanspec}

\subsection{Structure}
\Cref{fig:urban} shows the Urban HRU (\file{urban\_hru.json}). It has eight members:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \code{Catchment} --- the pervious (infiltrating) surface;
\item \code{Impervious\_Catchment} --- the impervious surface, with no connection to any soil;
\item \code{Reach}, \code{Impervious\_Reach} --- two \code{Trapezoidal Channel Segment}s that collect
      the runoff of each surface: the swales, gutters, small streams and storm pipes inside the unit;
\item \code{Soil\_1}, \code{Soil\_2}, \code{Soil\_3} --- three van Genuchten soil layers under the
      pervious surface;
\item \code{Groundwater} --- an \code{Unconfined Groundwater cell} (\cref{sec:unconfined}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent and six internal links: a \code{Catchment\_link} from each surface to its reach,
infiltration (\code{surfacewater\_to\_soil\_link}) from the pervious surface into \code{Soil\_1},
percolation (\code{soil\_to\_soil\_link}) from \code{Soil\_1} to \code{Soil\_2} and from
\code{Soil\_2} to \code{Soil\_3}, and recharge from \code{Soil\_3} into the groundwater cell.

\code{Reach\_link} and \code{Impervious\_Reach\_link} are defined in \file{urban\_hru.json}. Their
equations are those of the channel-to-channel connector (\code{Trapezoidal\_Channel\_link}); they exist
as separate types only so that the two outlets of the composite can be told apart when a link is drawn
from it, and they have their own icons so that HRU outlets are distinguishable from links along the
stream network. When a link is drawn from the unit in the interface, the port whose type matches the
chosen link is used; in a script, \code{create link;type=Reach\_link,from=SC01,to=R01} connects the
\code{Reach} member of \code{SC01}.

\subsection{Surface runoff and routing}
Rain falls on both surfaces. Water on a surface drains to its reach with the kinematic sheet-flow
relation of \code{Catchment\_link},
\begin{equation}
  Q_s = 86400\,\frac{W}{n_s}\,S^{1/2}\,\big(d-d_s\big)_+^{5/3},
\end{equation}
with overland width $W$ (\code{catchment\_width}), slope $S$ (\code{catchment\_slope}), Manning
coefficient $n_s$, ponded depth $d$ and depression storage $d_s$; $(x)_+=\max(x,0)$. Both surfaces
share $W$ and $S$ and have their own $n_s$ and $d_s$. The reaches drain to the receiving segment
through the outlet ports with Manning's equation driven by the head difference,
\begin{equation}
  Q_r = 86400\,\frac{1}{\bar n}\,A_u\,R^{2/3}\left(\frac{h_r-h_c}{\bar L}\right)^{1/2},
  \qquad \bar L=\tfrac12\,(L_r+L_c),\quad \bar n=\tfrac12\,(n_r+n_c),
  \label{eq:outlet}
\end{equation}
where $A_u$ is the flow area of the end with the higher head, $R$ the hydraulic radius from the
mean perimeter, and subscripts $r$ and $c$ refer to the internal reach and the receiving channel.
The reach bottoms are placed at the surface elevation.

\subsection{The impervious fraction}
\label{sec:impfrac}
The impervious surface drains directly to the internal impervious reach and from there to the
channel, with no chance to infiltrate. \code{impervious\_fraction} must therefore be the
\emph{directly connected} impervious area (DCIA) --- roofs, roads and parking areas whose runoff
reaches a storm drain or the stream without crossing pervious ground --- and not the total impervious
area (TIA) mapped from land cover. The composites take it as a plain number; how it is estimated is
up to the modeller. Common choices:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Empirical TIA--DCIA relations.} Sutherland (2000) gives DCIA as a function of TIA (both
  in percent, for TIA\,$\ge$\,1\,\%) for five degrees of connectivity:
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Drainage & DCIA \\
  \midrule
  Totally connected (curb, gutter and storm sewer; roofs tied in) & TIA \\
  Highly connected & $0.4\,\mathrm{TIA}^{1.2}$ \\
  Average (mostly storm-sewered, some roofs to lawns) & $0.1\,\mathrm{TIA}^{1.5}$ \\
  Somewhat disconnected & $0.04\,\mathrm{TIA}^{1.7}$ \\
  Highly disconnected (swales, roofs to lawns) & $0.01\,\mathrm{TIA}^{2}$ \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\item \textbf{Storm-drain inventories.} Where inlet locations are available, impervious surfaces
  within a set distance of an inlet (or of a guttered street that leads to one) can be counted as
  connected. Public inventories miss private systems, so this tends to be a lower bound.
\item \textbf{Calibration.} The estimate is scaled by
  \code{impervious\_multiplier} (\cref{sec:multipliers}); keeping each unit's estimate and
  calibrating one multiplier preserves the spatial pattern and adjusts the level.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Impervious surfaces that are \emph{not} directly connected remain part of the pervious
surface (\cref{sec:disconnected}).

\subsection{Areas}
With $f_i=\min(f_{imp}\,m_{imp},\,0.95)$, where $f_{imp}$ is \code{impervious\_fraction} and $m_{imp}$
the \code{impervious\_multiplier}:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Member & Plan area \\
\midrule
\code{Impervious\_Catchment} & $A\,f_i$ \\
\code{Catchment}, \code{Soil\_1}--\code{Soil\_3}, recharge interface & $A\,(1-f_i)$ \\
\code{Groundwater} & $A$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The aquifer continues beneath roads and roofs, so the groundwater cell receives the whole
area; it is recharged only through the pervious soil column. Were it given only the pervious area, the
water table would rise $1/(1-f_i)$ times too fast per unit of recharge --- twice as fast at
$f_i=0.5$ --- and any change of $f_i$ during calibration would also change the aquifer.

\subsection{Recharge through the unsaturated conductivity}
\label{sec:recharge}
\code{soil\_to\_unconfined\_groundwater\_link} (\file{unconfined\_groundwater.json}) connects
\code{Soil\_3} (the source, which must be a \code{Soil} block) to the groundwater cell:
\begin{equation}
  Q_r = A(1-f_i)\,K_{sat,3}\;
        \frac{k_r(S_{e,3})\,\big(h_3-\Delta z-h_g\big)_+ \;-\; \big(h_g-h_3+\Delta z\big)_+}{L},
        \qquad L=\tfrac12\,(d_3+b-\Delta z),
  \label{eq:recharge}
\end{equation}
with $d_3$ the thickness of \code{Soil\_3} and $b$ the \code{aquifer\_thickness} of the cell (the
unit's \code{groundwater\_thickness}), so that $L$ runs from the centre of the soil layer to the centre
of the aquifer cell, and $\Delta z$ the link's \code{elevation\_offset} (the unit's
\code{recharge\_elevation\_offset}, default 0). When the soil head is higher (drainage) the conductivity is the unsaturated conductivity of the bottom
layer, with the same Mualem $k_r$ as the percolation links; when the groundwater head is higher
(upward flow) the saturated conductivity is used.

The reason is the depth of urban and upland water tables. When the water table lies several metres or
more below the soil column, $h_3-h_g$ is dominated by elevation and hardly depends on how wet the soil
is. With a constant conductivity the flux is then almost independent of soil moisture: in a
watershed application with the water table 6--47\,m below the soil and $K_{rech}=0.1$\,m/d, the
constant-$K$ link gave a potential recharge of about 150\,mm/d at $S_e=0.5$, and at $S_e=0.2$ the
suction of the bottom layer reversed it into up to 380\,mm/d of upward flow from the deep water
table. Calibrating $K_{rech}$ can only trade one error for the other. With \eqref{eq:recharge} the
flux falls with $k_r$ as the bottom layer drains, and no separate recharge conductivity is needed.

\paragraph{Elevation offset.} The soil column of a unit sits at the unit's mean surface elevation,
while the single groundwater cell has its water table near the stream: in incised terrain the two can
be tens of metres apart, so that recharge falls across a large gap under a nearly constant gradient
and the water table can never reach the root zone. $\Delta z$ lets the recharge link evaluate
\code{Soil\_3} as if it lay $\Delta z$ lower, a chosen gap $G$ above the water table,
$\Delta z = \big(z_{3,\mathrm{bottom}}-h_{g,0}\big)-G$ (at least 0); the path length shrinks by the
same amount, exactly as if the column had been moved. Nothing else moves: the soil layers, the
catchments, the internal reaches and the groundwater cell keep their elevations, so surface routing
is unaffected. With a small $G$ the link reverses when \code{Soil\_3} dries (suction larger than
$G+d_3$), and evapotranspiration from the deeper layers can then draw on the groundwater.

\subsection{Unconfined groundwater}
\label{sec:unconfined}
The groundwater member is the stock \code{Unconfined Groundwater cell} of
\file{unconfined\_groundwater.json}, a cell with a free water table. Its storage $V_g$ and piezometric
head are related through the specific yield $S_y$ (the unit's \code{groundwater\_porosity}, which sets
both \code{specific\_yield} and \code{porosity} of the cell):
\begin{equation}
  h_g = z_g + \frac{V_g}{S_y\,A},\qquad V_{g,0}=S_y\,\big(h_0-z_g\big)\,A,
\end{equation}
where $z_g$ is the cell's \code{base\_elevation}, so the water table rises by $1/S_y$ per unit depth
of stored water. The initial state is the initial piezometric head $h_0$ (the unit's
\code{initial\_water\_table\_elevation}, same datum as \code{surface\_elevation}) and the initial
storage is computed from it; the initial water table therefore does not move when $S_y$ changes, for
example during calibration. (Specifying an initial moisture content instead would tie the initial head
to $S_y$: a calibrated value of 0.107 instead of 0.15 would raise it by 40\,\% of the saturated
thickness.) The cell reports its saturated thickness as \code{depth} $=h_g-z_g$. For the Urban HRU the
template gained one property, \code{aquifer\_thickness} (the unit's \code{groundwater\_thickness}): the
thickness of the cell from its base to the bottom of the soil column. It sets the recharge path
length of \eqref{eq:recharge}; it does not cap the storage.

Neighbouring units exchange groundwater through the stock \code{UC\_groundwater\_link}, a Darcy
connector whose transmissive thickness follows the water tables:
\begin{equation}
Q = \tfrac12\,(K_s+K_e)\;\tfrac12\,(b_s+b_e)\; W\,\frac{h_s-h_e}{L},
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the cells' \code{hydraulic\_conductivity} (the unit's \code{groundwater\_K}), $b$ their
saturated thickness (\code{depth}), $W$ the interface width (\code{width}, the contact length of the
shared boundary) and $L$ the distance between the centroids of the two units (\code{length}). For
example, \code{create link;type=UC\_groundwater\_link,from=SC01,to=SC02,width=3616,length=1352}.
The flow is multiplied by a drainage cutoff on the cell it leaves (the upgradient cell, $u$):
\begin{equation}
f_c=\min\!\Big(\frac{(b_u-b_{r,u})_+}{\delta},\,1\Big),
\end{equation}
with $b_r$ the cell's \code{residual\_thickness} (the unit's \code{groundwater\_residual\_thickness},
default 0) and $\delta$ the link's \code{cutoff\_depth} (default 0.05\,m). Lateral outflow therefore
tapers to zero as the cell drains to its residual thickness, and a cell cannot be emptied (or driven
negative) by its neighbours. Setting $b_r$ to the depth of the cell base below the receiving stream
bed keeps lateral flow from drawing the water table below the stream: with one cell per unit there
is no groundwater mound under the divide between two units, so the head difference across it is the
difference of their stream beds, and without the cutoff a high tributary unit can be drained into a
lower neighbour until its stream turns losing. Exchange with the stream itself is not cut off.

Baseflow uses \code{unconfined\_groundwater\_to\_stream}, the unconfined counterpart of
\code{groundwater\_to\_stream}:
\begin{equation}
Q_b = K_b\,A_b\,\frac{h_g-h_c}{L_b},
\end{equation}
with $h_c$ the head of the channel segment, $A_b$ the bank (seepage-face) area (\code{area}), $L_b$ the
flow path to the stream (\code{length}) and $K_b$ the link's \code{hydraulic\_conductivity}. Water
flows back into the aquifer when the stream is higher.

\subsection{Layer-specific soil}
\code{K\_sat\_}$i$, \code{alpha\_vG\_}$i$, \code{n\_vG\_}$i$, \code{theta\_sat\_}$i$ and
\code{theta\_res\_}$i$ ($i=1,2,3$) are set for each layer, for example from soil-survey horizons
averaged over the three depth intervals. The \code{K\_sat\_}$i$ go to each layer's
\code{K\_sat\_original}, so that the layer's \code{K\_sat\_scale\_factor} remains free for the
multiplier below.

\subsection{Evapotranspiration from the deeper soil}
\label{sec:et2}
Besides \code{Evapotranspiration} on \code{Soil\_1}, the Urban HRU has an optional source property
\code{Evapotranspiration\_2}, applied to \code{Soil\_2} and \code{Soil\_3} (the root zone below the
top layer). Each layer's withdrawal is scaled by its own $\theta/\theta_s$. Connecting a separate
source --- typically the same reference-ET series with its own \code{corr\_fact} --- lets uptake from
the deeper layers be scaled independently of the top layer, which gives summer evapotranspiration a
way to intercept water before it drains to the groundwater cell. Left empty, the deeper layers do not
transpire.

\subsection{Calibration multipliers}
\label{sec:multipliers}
All three default to 1 and leave the unit unchanged at that value.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,style=nextline]
\item[\code{impervious\_multiplier} ($m_{imp}$)] scales the impervious fraction:
  $f_i=\min(f_{imp}\,m_{imp},0.95)$. It is meant for calibrating the \emph{directly connected}
  impervious area watershed-wide when $f_{imp}$ comes from an empirical estimate. $m_{imp}>1$ means
  that more of the land drains straight to the channel than the estimate says (for example, more
  roofs and driveways tied to the storm drains); $m_{imp}<1$ means more of the impervious surface is
  disconnected. Area moves between the impervious catchment and the pervious catchment with its soil
  column; the total stays $A$ and the groundwater cell is unaffected. Infiltration, soil storage,
  \code{Soil\_1} evapotranspiration and recharge scale with the pervious area; the internal reaches and
  the \code{unconfined\_groundwater\_to\_stream} link do not change. $f_{imp}$ itself (for example a Sutherland
  estimate, \cref{sec:impfrac}) is fixed when the model is built; only $m_{imp}$ is calibrated.
\item[\code{K\_sat\_scale}] multiplies $K_{sat}$ of all three layers, and therefore also the
  recharge conductivity of \eqref{eq:recharge}.
\item[\code{K\_sat\_scale\_surface}] multiplies $K_{sat}$ of \code{Soil\_1} only: its
  \code{K\_sat\_scale\_factor} is \code{K\_sat\_scale}\,$\times$\,\code{K\_sat\_scale\_surface}. It
  controls infiltration at the surface --- reduced, for example, by the compaction of urban lawns and
  graded fill, which soil surveys of undisturbed profiles do not describe --- without changing
  percolation and recharge through the deeper layers. Note that the infiltration link drives flow with
  the suction gradient over half the top layer, so while \code{Soil\_1} is unsaturated its
  infiltration capacity is several times $K_{sat,1}$; infiltration-excess runoff from the pervious
  surface appears only when rain intensity exceeds that capacity.
\end{description}

\noindent Bind a model parameter to them at the composite level,
\begin{lstlisting}[style=plain]
create parameter;type=Parameter,name=p_imp,low=0.3,high=3,value=1,prior_distribution=log-normal
setasparameter; object=SC01, quantity=impervious_multiplier, parametername=p_imp
\end{lstlisting}
and the value reaches the members through the composite's mappings. When a parameter changes, the
member areas are re-evaluated and the initial soil storages $\theta_0A_\ell d_\ell$ recomputed, so the
initial moisture content stays $\theta_0$; the code generator (\code{ohq\_generate}) compiles the same
mappings and initial storages as expressions of the parameter vector.

\subsection{Disconnected impervious area}
\label{sec:disconnected}
Only the \emph{connected} impervious fraction is an impervious surface. Impervious surfaces that drain
onto lawns --- roofs with leaders to splash blocks, driveways sloping to grass --- belong to the
pervious catchment $A(1-f_i)$ and are treated as soil: rain on them can infiltrate, and the
\code{Soil\_1} evapotranspiration and the recharge interface act over them. Neither the concentration
of their runoff onto the adjacent lawn nor the absence of transpiration from roofs is represented. If
that matters, a third \code{Catchment} for the disconnected area, joined to \code{Catchment} by a
\code{Catchment\_link}, would represent both; it is not part of the composite.

\section{Dual drainage: units served by storm sewers}
\label{sec:dualdrainage}
In developed catchments the runoff of a unit often has no open channel to flow in: streets and
swales collect it, inlets take it into a storm-sewer trunk, and the trunk discharges into the nearest
stream. Only when the sewer is full does water stay on the street and flow overland. The template
\file{dual\_drainage.json} represents this \emph{dual drainage} with existing blocks and two new
links (\cref{fig:dualstructure}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a \emph{surface segment} (any open-channel block, normally a \code{Compound Channel Segment}
        shaped as the street or swale) receives the unit's \code{Reach\_link} and
        \code{Impervious\_Reach\_link};
  \item a \code{Sewerchannelsegment} (\file{Sewer\_system.json}) is the trunk sewer;
  \item \code{Swale\_to\_pipe\_inlet} moves water from the surface segment into the sewer, and
        \code{Pipe\_outfall\_link} discharges the sewer into the receiving open channel;
  \item the surface segment also keeps its ordinary channel link (e.g.\ \code{Compound\_Channel\_link})
        to the receiving channel, which carries the overflow when the inlets or the sewer cannot take
        the flow.
\end{itemize}
The template lists \file{main\_components.json}, \file{open\_channel.json},
\file{compound\_channel.json} and \file{Sewer\_system.json} in its \code{requires}. Nothing in the
existing templates changes.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{dual_drainage_structure}
\caption{Dual drainage of an Urban HRU: the unit drains to a surface segment, inlets take the water
into a sewer segment, the sewer discharges into the receiving channel, and flow the sewer cannot take
continues overland.}
\label{fig:dualstructure}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Inlets}
All inlets that drain the surface segment are lumped into one grate inlet with the two regimes of
FHWA HEC-22: weir flow while the water over the inlets is shallow and orifice flow once they are
submerged, whichever is smaller,
\begin{align}
Q_w &= C_w\,P\,|\Delta h|^{3/2}\,\frac{|\Delta h|}{|\Delta h|+h_s}, &
Q_o &= C_d\,A\,\sqrt{2g\,|\Delta h|}, &
Q &= \operatorname{sign}(\Delta h)\,\min(Q_w,Q_o),\\
\Delta h &= \max(H_{\mathrm{surf}},z_{\mathrm{in}})-\max(H_{\mathrm{sewer}},z_{\mathrm{in}}),
\end{align}
with $P$ the total weir length (\code{inlet\_perimeter}), $A$ the total effective opening
(\code{inlet\_area}), $C_w=1.66$ (SI) and $C_d=0.67$ (HEC-22 defaults), $z_{\mathrm{in}}$ the inlet
elevation (normally the bottom of the surface segment) and $H$ the hydraulic heads. Water enters
while the sewer head is below the surface water level. When the sewer surcharges above it the flow
reverses and the sewer overflows to the street; with both heads below the inlets the flow is zero. With \code{one\_way}~=~1 (default 0) $\Delta h$ is replaced by $\max(\Delta h,0)$:
the inlets only take water in, and water that rises above the street stays in the receiving block (for
a catch basin with a rim and spreading, below). The default is byte-identical to the two-way link.

The factor $|\Delta h|/(|\Delta h|+h_s)$ (\code{smoothing\_depth} $h_s$, default 5\,mm) matters
numerically, not physically (it is below 20\,\% for depths above $5h_s$). A pure orifice law has an
infinite derivative at zero depth, and a weir law $|\Delta h|^{3/2}$ still drains a surface segment
whose storage grows as depth$^2$ at an unbounded relative rate as it empties; both stalled the
solver in the example (the 15-day test took 111\,s instead of 3\,s). With the factor the inflow falls
off as depth$^{5/2}$ and the surface segment empties smoothly.

\subsection{Outfall}
The sewer segment's head is at its centre; the outfall link applies Manning's equation over half
its length with the sewer's flowing area $A_s$ and wetted perimeter $P_s$,
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{1}{n}\,\operatorname{sign}(\Delta h)\sqrt{\frac{|\Delta h|}{L/2}}\;A_s\Big(\frac{A_s}{P_s}\Big)^{2/3},\qquad
\Delta h = \max(H_{\mathrm{sewer}},z_{\mathrm{out}})-\max(H_{\mathrm{chan}},z_{\mathrm{out}}),
\end{equation}
with $z_{\mathrm{out}}$ the outfall invert (\code{outfall\_invert}). The outfall is free while the
receiving water surface is below the invert. A full sewer segment raises its head steeply with any
further storage (\code{Sewerchannelsegment}), which drives pressurised flow through the outfall.
Reverse flow (stream above the sewer) uses the sewer's current flowing area, so it cannot fill an
empty sewer from the stream.

\subsection{Sewer size, surcharge and groundwater}
\code{Sewerchannelsegment} has a \code{diameter\_multiplier} (default 1) that scales its diameter in
all of its hydraulics and in the links that read it (\code{Sewer2Sewer\_link}, \code{sewer2pond},
\code{soil2sewer}); it lets a calibration adjust uncertain pipe sizes with one parameter while each
segment keeps its own nominal diameter. With the default the results are unchanged (the example's
outputs are byte-identical to those before the property was added); with 1.5 the example's sewer
carries the whole second storm and the overland overflow nearly vanishes.

When a sewer is full, its head rises by \code{surcharge\_coefficient} (m per m$^3$) for every cubic
metre stored above the full pipe. The default, 10\,000, keeps the earlier behaviour of a practically
rigid pipe; it is very stiff for the solver, and the code-generated kernels, which give up after 20
failed attempts at a step, stop at the first surcharge. Real sewers surcharge into their manholes:
with manholes of plan area $A_m$ every $s$ metres the coefficient is $1/(A_m L/s)$, e.g.\ 0.11 for the
example's 800\,m sewer with 1.2\,m manholes every 100\,m. Once the head reaches the street the inlet
link returns the water to the surface. The example uses this value.

Groundwater that enters storm sewers (infiltration through joints and trenches) can be represented
with the Urban HRU's \code{unconfined\_groundwater\_to\_stream} link to the sewer segment: the flow is
$K A (H_{gw}-H_{sewer})/\ell$ with the sewer's head, so the sewer drains the aquifer while the water
table is above it and loses water to it only while it carries flow (the link is limited at zero
sewer storage; tested with the water table below and above the pipe).

\subsection{Catch basins with a rim and spreading}
The shafts of a sewer (manholes, inlet boxes) can be represented explicitly with \code{Catch basin}
blocks between the inlets and the sewer (street $\to$ \code{Swale\_to\_pipe\_inlet} $\to$ catch
basin $\to$ \code{Sewer\_pipe} $\to$ sewer). The block has a base area $A$ and bottom $z$, and two
optional properties: \code{max\_height} $h_{\max}$ (rim above the bottom) and
\code{spread\_side\_slope} $m$. Below the rim it is a prism; above it the plan area grows as an
inverted square pyramid, $A(e)=(\sqrt{A}+2me)^2$ at height $e$ above the rim, which stores water that
would spread over the surroundings. Its depth is
\[
d=\min\!\Big(\frac{S}{A},h_{\max}\Big)+\frac{3V}{X^2+X\sqrt{A}+A},\qquad
V=\max(S-Ah_{\max},0),\quad X=\big(A^{3/2}+6mV\big)^{1/3},
\]
the closed-form inverse of $V(e)=[(\sqrt A+2me)^3-A^{3/2}]/(6m)$, written so that it is exactly $S/A$
when $V=0$ and reduces to the prism when $m=0$. With the defaults ($h_{\max}=10^{12}$, $m=0$) the
block is the earlier prism: a model using catch basins gives identical results (185 of 185 series).
$\mathrm{d}d/\mathrm{d}S=1/A$ on both sides of the rim, so the transition is smooth. The block
reports \code{flood\_depth} ($e$), \code{flood\_volume} ($V$) and \code{spread\_area}
($A(e)-A$). In the example with a catch basin of 40\,m$^2$, rim 2\,m, $m=30$ and a sewer
\code{surcharge\_coefficient} of 10, the second storm raises the basin 0.16\,m above the rim
(20.7\,m$^3$, 211\,m$^2$); the generated kernel runs it without failure and matches the interpreter's
peak storages within 0.3\,\%. A catch basin alone does not remove the stiffness of a rigid sewer
(default \code{surcharge\_coefficient}): keep a finite coefficient on the sewer.

\subsection{Example}
\exfile{Examples/Dual\_Drainage/dual\_drainage\_example.ohq} is a 40\,ha Urban HRU (30\,\%

800\,m, 0.6\,m sewer that discharges into a 600\,m stream; the street also overflows to the stream.
Two storms test both regimes (\cref{fig:dualexample}; manhole surcharge storage, coefficient 0.11):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item 10\,mm/h for 3\,h: the inlets take all the street runoff (0.33\,m$^3$/s), the sewer runs
        62\,\% full and nothing flows overland;
  \item 50\,mm/h for 1\,h: the street receives 1.51\,m$^3$/s; the sewer surcharges (outfall
        0.65\,m$^3$/s at capacity, inlets briefly 0.75\,m$^3$/s while the sewer fills) and
        0.58\,m$^3$/s flows overland to the stream. The sewer head rises above the inlets but the
        street water surface stays higher, so no water returns to the street.
\end{itemize}
The run takes 3\,s for 30 days (8\,s with the rigid default). A kernel generated from the example
(\file{codegen}) reproduces the interpreter: peak storages of street, sewer and stream within 0.5\,\%,
time-averaged storages within 1--4\,\% (soils and groundwater within 0.05\,\%); with the rigid default
the kernel stops at the second storm. Volume balances of the street and the sewer computed from the
written outputs close within 0.3\,\% for the first storm. For the second they differ by 1--2\,\% at
the abrupt end of the rain, where flows change faster than the output interval; with a ten times
smaller time step the sewer's difference fell from 94 to 8\,m$^3$, i.e.\ it is an artefact of
integrating the interpolated outputs (the links move water between blocks within each step).

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item One lumped inlet per surface segment: the time water spends on the street before reaching an
        inlet and inlet bypass at high gutter velocities are not represented.
  \item One sewer segment per unit represents the trunk; the branch network's storage and travel
        time are lumped into it.
  \item The sewer cannot be filled from the receiving stream (see Outfall); backwater into the sewer
        is therefore underestimated during floods that rise above the outfall.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and pitfalls}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Fixed layer split.} The three soil layers are always $D/7$, $2D/7$ and $4D/7$; $D$
  controls the storage, the recharge path length and the heads together, so calibrating it moves all
  three at once.
\item \textbf{Deep water tables in the HRU.} When the water table is far below the soil column, the
  HRU's constant recharge conductivity drains the column regardless of its wetness and pulls water
  upward when the bottom layer dries (\cref{sec:recharge}); keep \code{recharge\_K} small or use the
  Urban HRU, whose recharge follows the unsaturated conductivity.
\item \textbf{Confined-style defaults in the HRU.} With $S_s=0.01$\,m$^{-1}$ the head responds
  metres to small changes of storage. For an unconfined aquifer set $S_s=\phi/b$.
\item \textbf{Outlet link length.} \eqref{eq:outlet} averages the length of the internal reach with
  that of the receiving segment, so a long receiving segment lowers the outlet slope.
\item \textbf{Internal reaches are generic.} Their shape and roughness are properties of the unit,
  not derived from its drainage network; they mostly control the timing of the runoff peak.
\item \textbf{Evapotranspiration.} The HRU transpires from \code{Soil\_1} only; the Urban HRU from
  \code{Soil\_1} and, with \code{Evapotranspiration\_2}, from \code{Soil\_2} and \code{Soil\_3}. Each
  layer's rate is scaled by its own $\theta/\theta_s$. The groundwater cell does not transpire.
\item \textbf{Hourly rainfall and infiltration excess.} With hourly forcing, short bursts of high
  intensity are averaged out, so infiltration-excess runoff from pervious surfaces is underestimated;
  \code{K\_sat\_scale\_surface} can compensate in calibration.
\item \textbf{Disconnected impervious area} is treated as pervious soil (\cref{sec:disconnected}).
\item \textbf{One unit, one soil column.} Spatial variation inside a sub-catchment is represented
  only through its areas; use more units for more variation.
\end{itemize}
