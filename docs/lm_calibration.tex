\section{Overview}

Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) estimates model parameters by walking downhill from a
starting guess to the best-fitting values, using the slope of the fit error to
choose each step. It minimises exactly the same negative log-likelihood that
\textbf{Inverse Run} (genetic algorithm) and \textbf{MCMC parameter estimation}
minimise, so the three methods are directly comparable and can be used in sequence on the
same model without changing anything about the model itself.

What distinguishes LM from its two siblings:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{It is cheap.} One iteration costs roughly one model run per
        calibrated parameter. A genetic algorithm costs population $\times$
        generations, typically 1600 runs at the default settings.
  \item \textbf{It reports uncertainty.} At the solution it produces a standard
        error and a 95\,\% interval for every parameter, plus the correlation
        matrix between them. The GA reports a point estimate and nothing else;
        MCMC reports uncertainty but needs thousands of runs to do it.
  \item \textbf{It is local.} It finds the optimum nearest its starting point.
        On a model with several distinct optima it will not search for the
        global one.
\end{itemize}

That last point defines the intended workflow. LM starts from each parameter's
\emph{current value}, and \textbf{Inverse Run} writes its estimates back into
the model's parameters when it finishes. Running LM immediately after it
therefore continues from the GA's answer with no configuration at all:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Inverse Run} finds the basin. \textbf{Levenberg-Marquardt} converges
inside it and quantifies the uncertainty.
\end{quote}

Used alone from a good starting guess, LM is often all that is needed. Used
alone from a poor one on a multi-modal problem, it will return a confident
answer about the wrong optimum, which is the one failure mode to keep in mind.

\section{What is computed}

\subsection{The objective}

Every \code{Observation} with observed data contributes a Gaussian negative
log-likelihood. For an observation compared at $n$ points,
\begin{equation}
  -\log L \;=\; \frac{n}{\tau}\left(\frac{\mathrm{MSE}}{2\sigma^{2}}
                                    + \log \sigma\right),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{MSE} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(o_i - m_i\right)^{2},
\end{equation}
where $o_i$ and $m_i$ are the observed and modeled values, $\sigma$ is the
observation's \code{error\_standard\_deviation}, and $\tau$ is its
effective-information scale. For a \code{log-normal} error structure the
residuals are taken in log space. The total objective is the sum over all
observations. This is the same quantity \code{System::CalcMisfit()} returns to
the GA and to MCMC.

\subsection{The residual vector}

LM does not minimise that scalar directly. It decomposes it into a vector of
weighted residuals
\begin{equation}
  r_i \;=\; \frac{o_i - m_i}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  -\log L \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{r}\rVert^{2} + n\,\log\sigma ,
\end{equation}
with an extra factor $\sqrt{w_i}$ per point under the
\code{Weighted Least Squared} kernel. Every scaling the misfit applies is folded
into $\mathbf{r}$, so the residual vector LM differentiates and the misfit the
GA minimises are two views of one quantity, computed by one piece of code.

\subsection{The step}

With $J_{ij} = \partial r_i / \partial \theta_j$ estimated by finite
differences, each iteration solves the damped normal equations
\begin{equation}
  \left(J^{\!\top}\! J + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(J^{\!\top}\! J)\right)\,
  \boldsymbol{\delta} \;=\; -J^{\!\top}\mathbf{r},
\end{equation}
and tries the point $\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\delta}$. Small $\lambda$
gives the full Gauss-Newton step, which converges quickly near the solution;
large $\lambda$ gives a short step down the steepest-descent direction, which is
safer far from it. The damping adapts automatically: it is reduced after a step
that improves the fit and raised after one that does not. Scaling by
$\mathrm{diag}(J^{\!\top}\! J)$ instead of by the identity makes the damping
insensitive to how the parameters are scaled, which matters when conductivities
and porosities are calibrated together.

Parameters are searched in the same space the GA uses: $\log_{10}$ for a
parameter whose \code{prior\_distribution} is \code{log-normal}, linear
otherwise, bounded by its \code{low} and \code{high}. Steps that would leave the
box are projected back onto it.

\subsection{Convergence}

The run stops at the first of these:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item the fit improves by less than \code{tol\_objective} (relative);
  \item the step is shorter than \code{tol\_step} (relative);
  \item the largest gradient component is below \code{tol\_gradient};
  \item no step improves the fit in \code{max\_rejections} attempts at heavier
        damping, which means a minimum has been reached to within the
        resolution the finite-difference gradient can see;
  \item \code{max\_iterations} is reached.
\end{itemize}

The stop reason is written in plain words at the end of \file{LM\_output.txt}.
Read it: ``reached the iteration limit'' means the run was cut short and should
be continued, while the others mean it converged.

\subsection{Parameter uncertainty}

At the solution $J^{\!\top}\! J$ is the Gauss-Newton approximation of the
likelihood's Hessian, so its inverse is the parameter covariance. Because the
residuals already carry $1/\sigma$, this is a covariance and not merely a
curvature. LM recomputes the Jacobian at the converged point (rather than
reusing the last iteration's, which was taken one step away) and reports:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item the standard error of each parameter, in the space it was searched in,
        so a \code{log-normal} parameter's error refers to $\log_{10}$ of its
        value;
  \item a 95\,\% interval, converted back to model units;
  \item the correlation matrix between parameters.
\end{itemize}

\begin{caution}
\textbf{Read the correlation matrix.} A pair of parameters correlated near
$+1$ or $-1$ cannot be told apart by the available data: the fit can trade one
against the other with no penalty. The individual standard errors will look
reasonable while the pair is jointly unidentified. Fix one of them, or add an
observation that constrains one alone.
\end{caution}

\subsection{Error standard deviations}

When a parameter drives an observation's \code{error\_standard\_deviation}, the
objective is no longer a pure sum of squares and $\sigma$ would have to be
searched for like any other parameter. LM avoids this: $\sigma$ has a closed
form at any point,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\sigma}^{2} \;=\; \mathrm{MSE},
\end{equation}
so LM sets it to that value at every iteration and drops it from the search.
This is exact, costs nothing, removes a parameter from the problem, and is the
same value the GA converges to. It is on by default (\code{profile\_sigma});
the estimate reported at the end is the profiled one.

\section{Requirements and limitations}

LM refuses to start, with an explanatory message, unless all of the following
hold. Nothing is run and no output is overwritten when it refuses.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The model defines observations with observed data.} LM needs
        residuals. It cannot score a design objective-function set, which is
        what \textbf{Optimize} is for; LM's counterpart is \textbf{Inverse
        Run}, the genetic algorithm scoring the same likelihood.
  \item \textbf{Every observation uses a comparison method with a
        sum-of-squares form.} \code{Least Squared}, \code{Weighted Least
        Squared} and \code{EMC} all qualify. \code{Similarity} does not: it is
        an autocorrelation and Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance with no
        Gauss-Newton form. Use \textbf{Inverse Run} for such a model.
  \item \textbf{The model solves at the starting parameter values.} LM has no
        population to fall back on. If the starting point fails, run
        \textbf{Inverse Run} first to reach a feasible region.
  \item \textbf{There are at least as many residuals as free parameters.}
        Otherwise the problem is underdetermined and the covariance is
        singular by construction.
  \item \textbf{The number of residuals does not change when a parameter is
        perturbed.} If it does, the comparison points themselves are moving
        with the parameters and a Jacobian is not defined. LM detects this and
        stops rather than differentiating nonsense.
\end{itemize}

\section{Settings}

The \textbf{LM} settings object holds the configuration. Every entry has a
usable default, so a model that has never been configured for LM will run.

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.25\textwidth}L{0.19\textwidth}L{0.50\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Default} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\code{max\_iterations} & 50 &
  Most Gauss-Newton steps attempted. Each costs about one model run per
  parameter. A well-behaved problem stops well inside 50 on its own. \\
\code{lambda0} & 0.01 &
  Initial damping. Small takes the full Gauss-Newton step, good when already
  close; large takes short downhill steps, safer from a poor start. It adapts
  after the first step, so this mostly affects the opening moves. \\
\code{fd\_step} & 0.001 &
  How far each parameter is nudged to measure its effect, as a fraction of that
  parameter's range. See \S\ref{sec:fdstep}. \\
\code{central\_differences} & No &
  Nudge each parameter both up and down. Twice the model runs per iteration,
  for a more accurate gradient. \\
\code{profile\_sigma} & Yes &
  Set a calibrated \code{error\_standard\_deviation} to its analytic best value
  each iteration instead of searching for it. \\
\code{lm\_numthreads} & 8 &
  Perturbed models solved at once while measuring the gradient. Nothing is
  gained from more threads than parameters, or than the machine has cores. \\
\code{tol\_objective} & 1e-8 &
  Stop when an iteration improves the fit by less than this fraction. \\
\code{max\_rejections} & 10 &
  How many times a failed step is retried with heavier damping before the run
  concludes it has reached a minimum. Each retry is a full model run. \\
\code{lm\_outputfile} & \texttt{\scriptsize LM\_output.txt} &
  Iteration log. \\
\code{covariancefile} & \texttt{\scriptsize LM\_covariance.txt} &
  Standard errors, intervals and the correlation matrix. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}


\noindent Further properties are settable from a script or the command
line but are not exposed in the dialog, because the defaults are almost always
right: \code{lambda\_up} and \code{lambda\_down} (both 3, the factors by which
damping is raised after a rejected step and lowered after an accepted one),
\code{lambda\_max} (1e12), \code{tol\_step} and \code{tol\_gradient} (both
1e-8), and \code{write\_covariance} (Yes).

\section{Using LM in the GUI}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Define the parameters to calibrate, with a \code{low}, a \code{high} and
        a \code{prior\_distribution}, and assign each to the model quantity it
        drives.
  \item Define at least one \code{Observation} with observed data.
  \item Set the parameters' \code{value} fields to your starting guess. This is
        where LM begins. If you have just run \textbf{Inverse Run}, they already
        hold the GA's estimates and there is nothing to do.
  \item Adjust the \textbf{LM} settings object if needed.
  \item Press the \textbf{Levenberg-Marquardt calibration} button in the general
        toolbar.
\end{enumerate}

The progress window plots the negative log-likelihood against iteration number.
The secondary progress bar tracks the perturbed model runs within the current
Jacobian. When the run finishes, the calibrated values are written back into the
model's parameters and the results are transferred in, so the plots show the
calibrated model. The log reports the iteration count, the number of model runs
consumed, and where the covariance file was written.

If LM refuses to start, a dialog explains which of the conditions in
\S3 was not met. Nothing has been run at that point.

\section{Using LM from the console}\label{sec:console}

\subsection{Script syntax}

LM settings are set like any other settings object, and the calibration is run
with the \code{lmoptimize} command:

\begin{lstlisting}
setvalue; object=LM, quantity=max_iterations, value=30
setvalue; object=LM, quantity=central_differences, value=Yes
setvalue; object=LM, quantity=numthreads, value=4
lmoptimize
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Because LM starts from the parameters' current values and
\code{optimize} writes its result back into them, the hybrid is simply the two
commands in order:

\begin{lstlisting}
optimize
lmoptimize
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Headless runner: \code{OHQ-LM}}

\begin{lstlisting}
cd terminal/OHQ-LM && mkdir -p build && cd build
qmake6 ../OHQ-LM.pro && make -j$(nproc)

./OHQ-LM model.ohq output_folder/
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Options, each overriding the model's \textbf{LM} settings when given
and leaving them alone when not:

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.34\textwidth}L{0.62\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Option} & \textbf{Effect} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\code{-{}-verify-residuals} & Check the likelihood decomposition and exit. See
  \S\ref{sec:verify}. \\
\code{-{}-iterations <n>}   & Maximum iterations. \\
\code{-{}-lambda0 <x>}      & Initial damping. \\
\code{-{}-fd-step <x>}      & Finite-difference step, as a fraction of range. \\
\code{-{}-central}          & Central differences. \\
\code{-{}-no-profile-sigma} & Keep a calibrated $\sigma$ in the search. \\
\code{-{}-threads <n>}      & Parallel model runs per Jacobian. \\
\code{-{}-kernel <lib.so>}  & Run the forward model from a codegen-generated
  shared library instead of the interpreter. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Checking the likelihood decomposes}\label{sec:verify}

\begin{lstlisting}
./OHQ-LM model.ohq output_folder/ --verify-residuals
\end{lstlisting}

This costs one forward solve and confirms that
$\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^{2} + \sum_k n_k\log\sigma_k$ equals
\code{CalcMisfit()} on this model, which is the identity the whole method rests
on. It also reports, per observation, how many points were actually compared,
the $\sigma$ in force, and that observation's contribution to the objective.

It is worth running once on any new model. A comparison count well below the
number of observed points means data lie outside the simulated time window and
are not contributing at all.

\subsection{\file{LM\_output.txt}}

A commented header records the settings, which parameters were optimised and in
which space, and which error standard deviations were profiled out. Then one row
per attempted step:

\begin{lstlisting}

0    , -2.0052167e+01,  1.0000e-02, start  , 2, 3.5, 0.40935204
1    , -5.3282375e+01,  3.3333e-03, accept , 0.92588741, 1.7318153, 0.21336468
2    , -1.4003940e+02,  1.1111e-03, accept , 0.63504485, 1.9755143, 0.038934657
3    , -2.4437852e+02,  3.7037e-04, accept , 0.69347977, 2.0090912, 0.0050330171

\code{status} is \code{accept} when the step reduced the objective and
\code{reject} when the damping had to be raised and the step retried. Parameter

reason, the final objective and the final estimates, with profiled parameters
marked as such.

\subsection{\file{LM\_covariance.txt}}

\begin{lstlisting}
parameter    , space  , estimate   , std_error   , ci95_low  , ci95_high
k_decay      , log10  , 0.6941473  , 0.0008034   , 0.691635  , 0.6966687
C0           , linear , 2.01154    , 0.0025069   , 2.006626  , 2.016453

# correlation matrix
             , k_decay  , C0
k_decay      ,  1.0000  ,  0.6908
C0           ,  0.6908  ,  1.0000
\end{lstlisting}

The \code{space} column matters: for a \code{log-normal} parameter the standard
error is that of $\log_{10}$ of the value, while the interval columns are
converted back to model units.

This file is not written when the curvature at the solution is singular. That is
itself a result: it means at least one parameter, or one combination of them,
the data cannot resolve.

\section{Worked example}

A first-order decay in a batch reactor, $C(t) = C_0 e^{-kt}$, run to $t=5$ with
51 observed points taken from the analytic solution. Two parameters are
calibrated against their true values of $k=0.7$ and $C_0=2.0$, plus the error
standard deviation:

\begin{lstlisting}
create parameter;type=Parameter,name=k_decay,low=0.05,high=5,value=2.0,
       prior_distribution=log-normal
setasparameter; object=decay_coefficient, quantity=base_value,
       parametername=k_decay

create parameter;type=Parameter,name=C0,low=0.5,high=6,value=3.5,
       prior_distribution=normal
setasparameter; object=Reactor (1), quantity=A:concentration, parametername=C0

create parameter;type=Parameter,name=sigma_obs,low=1e-4,high=10,value=1.0,
       prior_distribution=log-normal
setasparameter; object=exact_solution, quantity=error_standard_deviation,
       parametername=sigma_obs
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Both parameters start far from the truth, at $k=2.0$ and $C_0=3.5$.
Running the genetic algorithm and LM separately on this model:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Inverse Run (GA)} & \textbf{Levenberg-Marquardt} \\
\midrule
Settings          & 40 population, 40 generations & 50 iterations allowed \\
Model runs        & 1643 & \textbf{42} \\
$-\log L$         & $-242.168$ & $\mathbf{-244.879}$ \\
$k$ (truth 0.7)   & 0.6962 & 0.6941 \\
$C_0$ (truth 2.0) & 2.0170 & 2.0115 \\
$\sigma$          & 0.004759 & profiled \\
Standard errors   & none & yes \\
Correlation       & none & $\rho(k,C_0) = 0.69$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

LM reached a slightly better optimum in roughly one fortieth of the model runs,
and additionally reported that $k$ and $C_0$ are 69\,\% correlated. That
correlation is real and expected: in $C_0 e^{-kt}$ a larger $C_0$ can be partly
compensated by a faster $k$. It is the kind of thing a point estimate alone
will never tell you.

The residual gap between the estimates and the exact values of 0.7 and 2.0 is
the forward solver's discretisation error against the analytic solution, not a
failure of the calibration: LM has found the best fit of the \emph{discretised}
model, which is the right answer to the question actually posed.

\section{Practical notes}

\subsection{The finite-difference step}\label{sec:fdstep}

The forward model is solved with an adaptive time step, so the objective is only
piecewise smooth. A perturbation smaller than the solver's own error measures
numerical noise rather than a gradient. The default \code{fd\_step} of 0.001 of
each parameter's range clears this on typical settings.

If LM stalls at the first iteration with no step accepted, suspect the gradient
before concluding the model is at an optimum. In order of what to try:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Increase \code{fd\_step} to \code{0.01}.
  \item Tighten the solver tolerance (\code{nr\_tolerance}, \code{minimum\_timestep}).
  \item Turn on \code{central\_differences}.
\end{enumerate}

Conversely, if the calibration lands on a slightly different answer each time it
is run from the same starting point, the gradient is being contaminated by
solver noise and \code{central\_differences} is the remedy.

\subsection{Parameters that sit on a bound}

A parameter driven against its \code{low} or \code{high} is held there by the
projection, and its reported standard error is not meaningful: the likelihood is
not locally quadratic at a boundary. Widen the range and re-run. If it returns to
the same bound, the data are telling you the parameter wants to go outside the
range you allowed.

\subsection{Parameters the data cannot see}

A parameter the residuals do not respond to produces a zero column in the
Jacobian. LM holds such a parameter still rather than failing, and the singular
curvature at the end suppresses the covariance file. Check that the parameter is
actually assigned to a quantity that affects a modeled observation.

\subsection{Comparing objective values between runs}

The objective is a negative log-likelihood, so \emph{lower is better} and the
values are routinely negative once $\sigma$ is profiled to a small value. Values
are comparable between the GA, MCMC and LM on the same model, which is what
makes the table in the worked example meaningful. They are not comparable across
models with different observations or a different number of compared points.
